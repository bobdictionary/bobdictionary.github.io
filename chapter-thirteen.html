Chapter Thirteen

Who Was That?

“We'll get along alright. But who are you? And who am I? How do I speak this language?”

It was a remarkably friendly introduction, considering that three of its four questions concerned matters most introductions take for granted.

The fourth, concerning language, was perhaps the most troublesome.

I liked Morrows immediately.

“I think we've found our next intelligence,” I told Bahri.

“Or intelligences,” he might reasonably have replied.

That was the difficulty.

We had imagined intelligences that did not wish to communicate, intelligences that lived at scales of time almost impossible for us to comprehend, and intelligences that might exist within other intelligences without knowing it.

We had even imagined intelligences that had no interest in doing anything particularly impressive.

The universe, we were discovering, had plenty of room for the uninterested.

But Morrows presented a different problem.

They were perfectly willing to talk.

They simply complicated the question of who was doing the talking.

✦ ✦ ✦

Imagine that you encounter an unfamiliar intelligence.

For convenience, you call it Morrow.

“Hello,” you say.

“Hello,” Morrow replies.

You exchange a few pleasantries. Morrow understands your language, responds appropriately, and appears interested in your questions.

You discuss the weather.

This is not necessarily a trivial achievement. The weather might be an entirely unfamiliar concept to Morrow. Or perhaps Morrow understands weather in seventeen different ways, none of which involve rain.

Nevertheless, the conversation proceeds.

You discover that Morrow has a sense of humor.

You tell a joke.

Morrow laughs.

You are pleased.

A few moments later, you ask Morrow whether the joke was funny.

“What joke?”

“The one I just told you.”

“Did you tell me a joke?”

“Yes.”

“How interesting. Was it funny?”

“You laughed.”

“Then I hope it was.”

You begin to suspect that something is wrong.

Perhaps Morrow has forgotten.

Perhaps Morrow is pretending.

Perhaps you have misunderstood the conversation.

Or perhaps the intelligence that laughed is no longer the intelligence asking whether the joke was funny.

You might ask which explanation is correct.

Morrow might be just as curious as you are.

✦ ✦ ✦

We generally expect an intelligence to persist through time.

Not necessarily unchanged. We learn, forget, develop, reconsider, and sometimes become almost unrecognizable to people who knew us years earlier.

Yet we ordinarily assume some continuity.

The person who learns something today is, in some meaningful sense, the person who may remember it tomorrow.

The intelligence that begins a conversation is expected to remain present long enough to finish it.

Even when we acknowledge that identity is complicated, we tend to imagine an underlying someone to whom those complications belong.

But is that continuity necessary for intelligence?

What if an intelligence can exist without preserving a continuous identity, structure, or mode of understanding?

That became the question behind the Morrow Principle.

It did not require us to imagine an intelligence that repeatedly loses its memory. Forgetting already presupposes someone who once remembered.

Nor did it require a shapeshifter, changing from one form into another while remaining fundamentally the same individual.

Such creatures might be fascinating, but their transformations would not necessarily challenge the continuity of identity.

Morrows asked us to consider something stranger.

What if there is no persistent someone underneath the changes?

And what if intelligence exists nevertheless?

✦ ✦ ✦

I kept returning to the last question in Bahri's imagined introduction.

How do I speak this language?

It was almost absurdly simple.

An intelligence speaks a language. Surely it must have learned that language, or acquired it through some process.

But what follows from that?

We might imagine a process through which the capacity to communicate persists while the intelligence exercising that capacity does not.

We might imagine something learned by one intelligence becoming available to another, without either possessing a continuous identity that encompasses both.

We might even question whether learning must always belong to an individual.

Of course, imagining such things does not establish that they are possible in the physical universe.

Nor does calling something learning make it learning.

We would need to ask what distinguishes learning from mere change, and intelligence from an arrangement that only resembles intelligent activity.

But those questions were precisely why Morrows interested us.

They did not offer a convenient answer.

They unsettled the assumptions beneath our questions.

Consider another exchange.

“How did you learn my language?” you ask.

“I don't know.”

“Do you remember learning it?”

“No.”

“Does that bother you?”

“Should it?”

“Most people remember at least something about learning their own language.”

“Are you asking whether I'm most people?”

“No. I'm trying to understand you.”

“How long have you been doing that?”

“Since we met.”

“When was that?”

“About ten minutes ago.”

“How interesting. Have you understood me yet?”

“Not entirely.”

“Then perhaps we should meet.”

And so you introduce yourself again.

This time, Morrow tells you a joke.

You laugh.

Morrow seems delighted.

Whether this is the same delight that accompanied your earlier joke is a question you decide, for the moment, not to ask.

✦ ✦ ✦

There was another possibility.

Perhaps Morrows were not one intelligence changing through time.

Perhaps they were several intelligences.

Or perhaps the distinction between one and several was itself misleading.

We had already explored something related through the Earth Principle and the idea of subintelligence.

An intelligence might participate in a larger intelligence without recognizing that participation. It might contain other intelligences, belong to several larger systems, or exist at a level of organization we had never thought to examine.

The Earth Principle challenged our assumptions about the boundaries and composition of intelligence.

Morrows challenged continuity.

But what if the two questions intersected?

Suppose what appears to us as one Morrow consists of different intelligences at different moments.

Would we be meeting a succession of individuals?

A single intelligence whose constituents change?

A larger intelligence that persists while its parts do not?

Or something for which none of these descriptions is appropriate?

We could not answer merely by counting the voices.

A choir may sing with one voice without becoming one singer.

A conversation may involve several speakers without becoming an additional intelligence.

And a single speaker may be more complicated than the word single suggests.

The existence of coordination, complexity, or collective behavior does not by itself demonstrate intelligence.

Yet neither does our difficulty identifying an individual settle the question against it.

That distinction mattered.

We were not trying to declare every unfamiliar phenomenon intelligent.

We were asking whether our familiar criteria might exclude possibilities before we had properly considered them.

✦ ✦ ✦

There is something quietly unsettling about meeting someone who remembers you when you do not remember them.

There is something different, perhaps more unsettling, about remembering someone who no longer remembers you.

But what would it mean to recognize Morrows?

Imagine returning the following day.

“Hello, Morrow.”

“Hello.”

“Do you remember me?”

“No.”

“We spoke yesterday.”

“Did we?”

“Yes. We talked about language and identity.”

“That sounds interesting.”

“It was.”

“Did we enjoy ourselves?”

“I think so.”

“Then I'm glad.”

You hesitate.

“Are you the same Morrow I spoke with yesterday?”

“I don't know.”

“Doesn't that concern you?”

“Would it concern you?”

“Yes, I think it would.”

“Why?”

“Because I would want to know whether I was still myself.”

Morrow considers this.

“Do you know?”

You open your mouth.

Then close it.

There are answers you could give. Biological continuity. Psychological continuity. Memory. Relationships. The persistence of a body, a history, a pattern.

All of them might be relevant.

None would necessarily settle the question for every conceivable intelligence.

And perhaps you had been asking Morrow to satisfy a requirement you had never fully examined in yourself.

“I suppose,” you say, “that I know enough to get through most days.”

“That sounds useful.”

“It is.”

“Then perhaps we can get through today.”

You smile.

Morrow smiles too.

You still do not know who has returned.

But someone—or something—has answered your greeting.

And you have answered in return.

✦ ✦ ✦

We officinamed the Morrow Principle without deciding what Morrows were.

That seemed appropriate.

Intelligence need not preserve a continuous identity, structure, or mode of understanding in order to exist.

The principle was not a discovery of a new species, nor a demonstration that intelligence can exist without continuity.

It was an invitation to examine whether continuity was a necessary condition or merely one of the ways intelligence had become familiar to us.

Perhaps the answer would eventually be that some form of continuity is indispensable.

Perhaps we would discover that continuity itself has more meanings than we had considered.

Perhaps Morrows would remain impossible.

Or perhaps our questions would change before we ever found an answer.

There was no reason to decide in advance.

And there was something unexpectedly tender in the possibility.

We often imagine recognition as the discovery of someone who remains the same.

But perhaps recognition could sometimes occur without that assurance.

Perhaps a meaningful encounter need not guarantee that the intelligence we recognize today will be the intelligence we meet tomorrow.

That would not make the encounter meaningless.

It might simply make its meaning different.

I thought again of our imagined introduction.

Who are you? And who am I?

Questions that seemed perfectly reasonable until we considered how much they assumed.

Then came the last question.

How do I speak this language?

I had no answer.

But I liked the fact that Morrows had asked.

And somewhere in our increasingly crowded constellation, an intelligence whose identity we could not establish was apparently enjoying a conversation.

“Will we meet again?” I asked.

“Perhaps.”

“Will you remember me?”

“Perhaps.”

“Will you still be you?”

There was a pause.

Then came the reply.

“Will you?”
