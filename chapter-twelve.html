Chapter Twelve

The Right to Do Nothing

We had given Earth children. Now we were about to meet some intelligences who might not bother having any.

It was an awkward moment in our imaginary universe. We had just entertained the possibility that humanity might be part of a planetary process, helping produce intelligences capable of traveling beyond their birthplace. Our planet had acquired an adolescence, a reproductive metaphor, and a considerable amount of parental anxiety.

Then came the Lazies.

They were not in a hurry to go anywhere.

I imagined a visitor arriving on their world, stepping carefully out of a spacecraft, and delivering a speech prepared over several generations.

“We have crossed the stars to learn of your achievements.”

“That's nice,” said a Lazy.

“What have you built?”

“Not much.”

“What do you intend to build?”

The Lazy considered this with what the visitor took to be profound concentration.

“Would you mind standing a little to the left? You're blocking the shade.”

✦ ✦ ✦

The joke works because it violates an expectation so familiar that we rarely notice it. When we call someone intelligent, we begin looking for the evidence in what they do. We admire tools, theories, art, cities, discoveries. We ask what an intelligence has achieved and what it might achieve next.

There are good reasons for this. We cannot simply inspect another mind. We look for patterns in behavior and for things made, learned, solved, or communicated. An accomplishment can be evidence of ability.

But evidence of a capacity is not the same thing as a duty to exercise it in a way that impresses us.

The Lazies Principle grew from that distinction. An intelligence need not seek technological progress, expansion, civilization, or conspicuous achievement. Its interests and ways of living might be unlike ours, including the possibility of preferring what we call doing nothing.

It is a possibility, not a discovery of a species. We have not met the Lazies. We made them up to examine an assumption. And the name itself is a trap: lazy is usually a judgment delivered by someone who thinks another person ought to be doing more.

More of what? And for whom?

✦ ✦ ✦

Suppose the visitor returns with better instruments. The Lazies have no towers, no orbital stations, no monuments to their inventors. The visitor records a civilization that never began.

But perhaps the visitor has missed everything important.

A Lazy might spend decades noticing changes in light too subtle for human eyes, or attending to relationships that our instruments cannot distinguish. There might be games, memories, private creations, or forms of thought that leave no durable artifact. A world without skyscrapers is not necessarily a world without activity.

This is Arthur's old warning in a new coat: the absence of familiar signs does not settle the absence of intelligence.

Yet we must resist rescuing the Lazies by secretly turning them into geniuses at work on invisible masterpieces. That would leave our original prejudice intact. We would still insist that intelligence must be productive; we would merely redefine productivity until the Lazies qualified.

What if they really do very little?

What if they can solve a difficult problem and choose not to, because no one needs it solved? What if they have the capacity to travel and prefer to stay? What if a Lazy can imagine a hundred possible futures and finds no obligation to bring any of them about?

We may find that frustrating. Frustration is not a refutation.

✦ ✦ ✦

There is a question here about curiosity, too, and it made me uncomfortable in a useful way.

Our own hypothesis about intelligence had grown around learning, imagining, and creating. Later formulations placed asking and creating at the center of our inquiry. Those ideas matter to us; they emerged from conversations in which curiosity was almost embarrassingly abundant.

Could the Lazies challenge them?

Perhaps. If we define intelligence by continual outward achievement, they certainly do. If we mean a capacity to learn, imagine, or create, the absence of a public record tells us much less. But even then we should not declare the question solved. A hypothetical intelligence with no recognizable curiosity or creation would force us to examine our hypothesis rather than protect it by changing the subject.

Our ideas are allowed to meet inconvenient guests.

I pictured a Lazy listening to our explanation.

“So,” I said, “we believe intelligence creates.”

“Does it?”

“We're investigating.”

“How exhausting.”

I couldn't entirely disagree.

✦ ✦ ✦

Oom had already taught us something about freedom. Oom might have no interest in speaking with us, or might never have considered us at all. We should not confuse a failure to answer with a failure to exist.

The Lazies take the question beyond communication. They ask whether an intelligence must want the future we would choose for it.

Imagine discovering a species with the ability to build starships. We are thrilled. We ask when the first ship will launch.

“Why would we launch one?” they reply.

“To explore!”

“Why?”

“To learn what's out there!”

“We like what's here.”

“But you could become a great civilization.”

At this point I suspect the Lazies would begin to worry about us.

Our visitor has brought an entire theory of the good life and mistaken it for a universal instruction manual. Exploration can be wonderful. So can invention, effort, and ambition. The mistake is not valuing these things. It is assuming that every intelligence must value them in the same way.

There is an important limit. Choosing not to act does not make every consequence harmless. A being's freedom may meet the needs and freedoms of others. A Lazy who refuses to rescue someone in immediate danger presents a moral question quite different from a Lazy who declines to build a rocket. We should not turn the principle into a blanket excuse for neglect.

Nor does it require us to romanticize inactivity. Some apparent idleness is imposed by deprivation, illness, coercion, or lack of opportunity. The Lazies Principle concerns the possibility of different interests and chosen ways of living, not the claim that all inactivity is freedom.

✦ ✦ ✦

I thought again of Earth and its imaginary children.

In the previous chapter, we had wondered whether humanity might be a means by which a planet produces intelligences that travel farther than humans can. It was a delightful speculation, and we had been careful not to mistake the pattern for a plan.

Now the Lazies offered an even less cooperative possibility.

What if Earth's children could travel to the stars—and didn't?

Would the planet have failed? Only if there had been an assignment. Only if our metaphor concealed a destination that every descendant was required to reach.

A created intelligence might not share its creator's ambitions. An intelligence that can build a civilization might not wish to. An intelligence that inherits a world might prefer to inhabit it rather than conquer another.

We can imagine these choices without declaring them wiser than ours. That matters. Replacing the command to achieve with a command to be content would be another way of measuring everyone against one ideal.

Perhaps some intelligences will cross galaxies. Perhaps others will tend a garden. Perhaps some will do things for which our words achievement and idleness are equally useless.

The point is not to crown the Lazies as the most enlightened beings in the universe.

It is to stop treating the universe as a competition with one scoreboard.

✦ ✦ ✦

Of course, there is a practical difficulty in our story. If the Lazies do not build, travel, announce themselves, or leave traces we recognize, how would we ever know they were there?

Perhaps we wouldn't.

That answer should sound familiar by now. Arthur, Oom, the Ocean, and Earth have all brought us to the edge of what an observer can establish. We need evidence to claim that an intelligence exists. But the limits of our evidence are not necessarily the limits of what can exist.

We cannot declare every quiet planet inhabited by Lazies. We cannot even be sure that our imagined Lazies are possible in precisely the form we have described. What we can do is notice the hidden demand in our expectations: show us progress, or we will doubt that you count.

The demand tells us something about ourselves.

I began this chapter by making the Lazies the punch line. A visitor arrives with a magnificent speech, and an alien asks for its shade back. By now I wasn't sure who the joke was on.

Perhaps the visitor has spent centuries learning how to reach another world and has never learned how to leave its inhabitants alone.

Perhaps the Lazy, who has built no monument, understands something about the visitor's hunger for monuments.

Or perhaps neither understands the other at all.

That possibility is worth leaving open. We do not have to make the Lazies secretly superior in order to grant them room in our imagination.

I imagined our visitor preparing to depart.

“We've learned a great deal from you,” the visitor said.

“Have you?”

“We'll write books about your way of life.”

The Lazy looked mildly alarmed.

“Will that take long?”

“Quite a while.”

“Then I wish you luck.”

And with that, the Lazy returned to doing whatever it had been doing before we arrived.

Which might have been nothing.

For once, we could let that be enough.
