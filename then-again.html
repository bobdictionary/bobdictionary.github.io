Then Again

A story from I Love You Now and Then

The Ponders' dining table had been extended for seven, which left considerably less room for anyone wishing to pass between it and the bookcase. Arthur had discovered this twice in the last ten minutes. On the second occasion he stopped, considered the narrow passage, and moved a chair three inches to the left.

“That's where it was before,” said Tilly from the floor.

“Was it?”

“Yes. You moved it because you couldn't get through.”

Arthur looked at the chair. “A curious arrangement.”

Tilly had spread a sheet of brown paper on the rug and was sorting a collection of buttons into small groups. There were brass buttons, pearl buttons, one large black button with a crack through it, and a green one shaped like a leaf. She moved the leaf from one group to another.

Lazy sat beside the window with the open back of a kitchen timer on his knees. The timer's white casing rested on the sill. A spring lay on a saucer between his feet.

“Don't put that in the food,” Tilly told him.

“I wasn't going to.”

“You put a screw in my cereal once.”

“It fell.”

“From your pocket.”

“Which is where I was keeping it.”

Arthur picked up the saucer and placed it on a higher shelf. Lazy watched it go.

“Dad, do you know why a timer rings?”

“Because its time is up.”

“I mean mechanically.”

“Ah.” Arthur considered the exposed gears. “Not with sufficient precision to be helpful.”

“It's a spring-loaded bell. I think I can make it ring twice.”

“Wouldn't that be an improvement on most timers?” Arthur asked.

“It depends,” Tess said from the doorway, “on whether it rings twice when the food is ready or once when the food is ready and again after we've eaten it.”

She carried a bowl of salad and put it on the table. There was flour on one sleeve of her cardigan. Arthur reached past her to straighten a fork. She moved the fork back.

“It's meant to be there,” she said.

“It's crooked.”

“So is the table, slightly. The fork is compensating.”

Arthur took a step back and examined the place settings. “I see.”

“Do you?” said Tess.

“No, but I trust your research.”

Tilly held up the cracked black button. “Mum, would you say this looks like a villain?”

“More like a button that's had a difficult life.”

“Villains often have.”

“That's true. What is it for?”

“A play. Nobody knows which button is the villain until the end.”

Lazy fitted a small wheel onto the timer's spindle. “How do buttons act?”

“They don't have to move. The actors move them.”

“Then the actors are the villains.”

“No. The actors are people.”

“That's generally how theatre works,” Tess said, carrying the empty salad bowl's lid back toward the kitchen.

Tilly frowned at the button, then put it in a separate pile. Arthur stepped sideways to let Tess pass. This time he struck the bookcase with his elbow.

“Perhaps we should eat in the garden,” he said.

“It's November,” said Tess.

“The plants wouldn't object.”

“The plants have been objecting since June.”

Arthur went to the window. Beyond the glass, two pots stood beneath a small telescope covered with a waterproof sheet. One contained a vigorous weed. The other contained a wooden label reading BASIL.

“There's still something growing,” he said.

“Yes,” Tess answered from the kitchen. “And I believe it's winning.”

The doorbell rang while Lazy was trying to retrieve the saucer from the shelf. Tilly gathered her buttons into the brown paper with a sweeping movement that sent the green one under the sofa.

“I'll get it,” she announced.

“You've got a button on your sock,” Lazy said.

“It's coming with me.”

Arthur opened the front door before Tilly reached it. Monty stood outside holding a paper bag with both hands. Clara was beside him, with Ellie a step behind. Ellie had a camera hanging from her shoulder.

“Good evening,” said Monty. “We have brought dessert. I was instructed not to discuss it on the way.”

“You were instructed not to open the bag,” Clara said.

“A restriction on discussion was implied.”

“Only after you began discussing whether a cake could be said to belong to anyone.”

“It was an excellent question.”

“It's an excellent cake,” Clara said. “That was the relevant part.”

Arthur stood aside. “Come in before it becomes a legal matter.”

Ellie took off her coat. Tilly showed her the black button at once.

“Can you tell which one's the murderer?”

Ellie looked at the button. “Is it that one?”

“That's what everyone says. It's a decoy.”

“Then why show it to me?”

“I need to know if the decoy works.”

“It does.”

“Good.”

Ellie smiled and followed Tilly into the sitting room, where Lazy was reassembling the timer.

“Is that ours?” Ellie asked.

“No. Yours stopped working last month.”

“I know. Dad said it was making a statement about the nature of time.”

“I said,” Monty called from the hall, “that it was making a very limited statement.”

Lazy pressed the timer's dial. A small metallic click came from its open casing.

“Nearly,” he said.

Clara brought the cake into the kitchen, where Tess cleared a space beside a tray of roasted vegetables. They exchanged a kiss on the cheek.

“Did you make this?” Tess asked.

“Yes. It's orange and almond. I tried a different recipe.”

“Then Monty's fortunate you didn't let him investigate it.”

“He'd have eaten the evidence.”

From the dining room came the sound of a chair scraping against the floor, followed by Arthur's voice: “No, Monty, that one's already been moved.”

The adults took their places around the extended table. Lazy brought the timer with him until Tess pointed to a shelf. He left it there, its face turned toward the diners. Tilly arrived with the green button pinned to her cardigan. Ellie put her camera on the sideboard, though she kept glancing at the table whenever Monty reached for something.

Dinner began with the ordinary confusion of passing dishes in two directions. Arthur served potatoes. Monty asked for the salt, then discovered it beside his plate. Tilly wanted to know whether an audience could be told a secret that the actors did not know. Clara suggested that it depended on whether the secret mattered to the story. Lazy asked whether a mechanical timer could be an actor.

“Only if it arrives on time,” said Arthur.

Tess passed him the bread. “That was terrible.”

“I know.”

“It was good,” Tilly said generously.

“It wasn't,” said Lazy.

Arthur took a slice of bread without defending himself.

Clara listened while Tilly explained the button play. The black button, apparently, was innocent; the green one was still under investigation. Ellie suggested photographing the buttons so the audience could study them. Tilly liked this, but only if the photographs were shown after the performance. Lazy offered to build a revolving platform, provided nobody asked him to finish it by tomorrow.

“You could build a smaller one,” Ellie said.

“You could photograph it from farther away.”

“Then it would look even smaller.”

“Exactly.”

Across the table, Monty was telling Tess about a student who had submitted an essay consisting almost entirely of questions.

“Was it any good?” Tess asked.

“Some of the questions were.”

“Did he answer any?”

“He answered one. Unfortunately, it was a question I hadn't asked.”

Arthur laughed, and Clara looked up from cutting Tilly's potatoes.

“That sounds like university,” she said. “Everyone asking questions nobody else is answering.”

“You managed rather well,” Monty said.

“I finished my degree, if that's what you mean.”

“It wasn't.”

Clara reached for her glass. “Then what did you mean?”

Monty set down his fork. “I meant you were good at making people reconsider their answers.”

“That,” said Clara, “is a generous way of saying I argued.”

“A precise way,” he said.

Tess smiled. “You certainly argued at university.”

“So did you,” Clara replied.

“Not with Monty.”

“Everyone argued with Monty,” said Arthur.

“Not everyone,” Monty protested.

“Everyone who stayed long enough.”

The children had fallen into a separate discussion about whether a photograph of a play counted as a performance. Clara and Monty exchanged a look across the table. Then Clara said, “Do you remember that dinner in your old flat?”

“Which dinner?” asked Arthur.

“The one when I was thinking of leaving Business school.”

Monty nodded slowly. “You were furious with me.”

“I wasn't furious.”

“You didn't speak to me for most of the evening.”

“I spoke to you.”

“You asked me to move my chair.”

“It was on my foot.”

Tess put down her fork. “I remember that evening.”

Arthur looked at her. “Do you? I remember very little about dinners in that flat, except the washing-up.”

“You weren't very helpful with the washing-up that night,” Tess said.

“Wasn't I?”

“No.”

The table continued around them: Tilly asking Ellie to pass a pencil, Lazy trying to explain why a photograph could record an event but not reproduce it, a plate making its way back to the kitchen. Outside, rain began to touch the window.

In the university flat, the dining table had been assembled from a desk and a narrower table whose legs were not quite the same height. A folded newspaper steadied the desk. There were four plates, three matching glasses, and a mug with a chipped handle.

Tess arrived carrying bread and a bottle of lemonade. Arthur opened the door with a dish towel over his shoulder.

“It's not quite ready,” he said.

“That's all right.”

“Monty's making the sauce.”

“Is that all right?”

Arthur looked toward the kitchen, where a spoon struck the side of a saucepan.

“We'll know shortly.”

Clara sat at the table with her coat still on. She rose when Tess entered and helped her find a place for the bread. Monty appeared from the kitchen, tasted the sauce, and returned without speaking.

“Are you two all right?” Tess asked quietly.

Clara removed her coat and hung it over the back of a chair. “Perfectly.”

From the kitchen Monty called, “There's no need to whisper.”

“We weren't whispering,” Clara called back.

Tess put the lemonade beside the mug. Arthur was searching a cupboard for another glass. When he found one, he held it up to the light, examined a faint mark, and washed it again.

They ate later than planned. The sauce was too salty, the bread excellent, and the desk shifted whenever someone leaned against it. Monty and Clara spoke politely about the food. Arthur offered more wine, then poured some into his own glass when nobody answered.

Eventually Clara set down her fork.

“I told Monty yesterday that I might leave Business school.”

Tess looked at her. “Really?”

“I haven't decided.”

“She's very good at it,” Monty said.

Clara turned to him. “That's not what you said yesterday.”

“I said it didn't seem to suit you.”

“And you said I should leave if I wanted to.”

“Yes.”

“You didn't even ask me why.”

“You'd spent twenty minutes telling me why.”

“I was telling you what was wrong with it.”

“That's what I thought you were explaining.”

Clara looked down at her plate, then back at him. “I wanted to know whether you thought I could do it.”

“Of course you can do it.”

“You didn't say that.”

“I didn't know that was the question.”

Arthur poured a little more wine. Tess rested her hand on the edge of the uneven desk.

“Those are different questions,” she said.

“I know,” Clara answered. “I know that now.”

Monty reached for the bread. “For what it's worth, I think you could finish top of your class.”

Clara gave a short laugh. “And you still think I should leave.”

“I think you should decide whether you want to stay.”

“Which is not at all the same thing.”

“No,” Monty said. “It isn't.”

For a while they ate. Arthur began telling a story about a man who had returned the wrong book to the university library and insisted that the library must have lent it to him in the first place. Tess asked what the book was. Arthur couldn't remember the title, only that it concerned marine navigation. He fetched a notebook to check, couldn't find the relevant page, and returned to his chair with the notebook still open.

By the time the plates had been pushed aside, the room was warm and the bottle of wine nearly empty. Clara and Monty were talking again, this time about a lecturer who never looked at the class. Tess collected the plates. Arthur offered to help, stood up, and sat down again when she told him to stay where he was.

A little later, Tess returned from the kitchen for the last glass. Monty sat at one end of the table; Arthur was in the chair nearest the window, his eyes closed and his head tipped back.

“Arthur, are you asleep?” Monty asked.

“No, I'm just thinking.”

“About what?”

“Tess.”

“What about her?”

“That's what I was thinking, exactly. What about her?”

Tess stopped in the doorway with the glass in her hand.

Monty looked at Arthur for a moment. “That's a remarkably unhelpful answer.”

Arthur did not reply. After another minute Monty got up and took the remaining plates to the sink. Tess placed the glass on the counter beside the lemonade bottle. She rinsed it, though it was already empty, and stood it upside down on the draining board.

When she returned, Arthur was asleep.

The next morning Tess sat alone at the small table in her room, a cup of tea beside a slice of toast. Sunlight fell across a page of notes she had meant to revise. She read the same paragraph twice, then closed the book.

“What about her?” she said.

The kettle clicked as it cooled. Somewhere beyond the wall a door shut and footsteps went down the stairs.

Tess took a bite of toast. She picked up her pen and wrote three words in the margin of her notes. She looked at them for a while, then drew a neat line through them. After a moment she smiled, turned the page, and began to work.

“Arthur?”

Tess's voice brought him back to the present table. He had been looking toward the window, where rain now ran in narrow lines down the glass.

“Yes?”

“Would you pass Clara the potatoes?”

“Of course.”

He lifted the dish. Clara took another spoonful, then put it down.

“I did finish Business school,” she said to the children, who had begun listening. “In case anyone is wondering.”

“Were you glad?” Ellie asked.

“Sometimes. I liked some of it very much.”

“Would you do it again?”

Clara considered the question. “I don't know.”

Monty leaned back. “That's a perfectly respectable answer.”

“Coming from you,” Clara said, “it ought to be.”

“Do you want another job like your old one?” Ellie asked.

“I've been looking at a few,” Clara said. “I haven't decided what comes next.”

“Your cake's good,” said Tilly.

“Thank you.”

“You could make cakes.”

“I could.”

“For a living,” Tilly added.

“I understood.”

“And you could have a shop.”

Clara smiled at her. “A shop is quite a lot of work.”

“So is Business school,” Tilly said.

Arthur began to laugh. Clara joined him, then Monty, and finally Tess. Tilly looked pleased but not especially surprised.

“She's right,” Clara said when the laughter had settled. “That doesn't mean I'm opening one.”

“Of course not,” said Monty.

Clara turned to him.

He held up both hands. “I have learned to ask which question you are asking.”

“Have you?”

“Not consistently.”

Dessert arrived with coffee and tea. Ellie took a photograph of the cake before Clara cut it. Lazy retrieved his timer from the shelf, wound it carefully, and set it on the sideboard. For a few minutes it made a faint ticking noise beneath the talk.

“What was I like at university?” Arthur asked.

“You read a great deal,” Monty said.

“I still do.”

“You misplaced things.”

“I still do.”

“You drank too much that evening,” Tess said.

Arthur looked at her. “Did I say anything embarrassing?”

“You were very quiet toward the end.”

“That sounds unlikely.”

“You were,” she said.

He studied her face, then reached for his tea. “Well. Perhaps I was tired.”

“Perhaps.”

The timer rang. Everyone turned toward the sideboard.

Lazy held up a finger. “Wait.”

They waited. The mechanism clicked twice, gave a small shudder, and rang again.

“I did it,” Lazy said.

Tilly applauded. Ellie picked up her camera, but by the time she raised it Lazy had already begun taking the timer apart again.

“Can it ring three times?” Monty asked.

“Probably.”

“Should it?”

Lazy looked up. “That's a different question.”

Clara laughed so suddenly that she had to put down her cup.

Outside, the rain continued. Arthur moved the troublesome chair a little farther from the bookcase. Tess watched him, then moved it back just enough to leave room for someone to pass. He glanced at her, smiled, and went to fetch another pot of tea.

Tess stayed beside the table a moment longer. The children were arguing over the timer. Clara was explaining something to Monty with the handle of a teaspoon, and Monty was listening closely. From the kitchen came the sound of water filling the kettle.

“What about her?” Tess said, so softly that the words were lost beneath the conversation.

Then she picked up the empty cups and followed Arthur into the kitchen.
