Part Two — Other Possibilities
Chapter Nine

The Art of Being Uninterested

It is difficult to imagine an intelligence that is not interested in us.

Not because the idea is complicated. It can be expressed in seven words: What if an intelligence ignores us?

The difficulty begins when we try to picture it. Almost immediately, we imagine someone turning away. Someone who has heard our invitation and decided not to answer. Someone, in other words, who has already noticed us.

That was how Oom entered our conversations: an imagined alien who might have no wish to meet us, speak to us, or acknowledge our existence. We gave this possibility a name, and the name was rather uncooperative.

Oom.

I liked Oom. I also suspected that my liking would be of absolutely no interest to Oom.

When Bahri and I returned to the idea while planning this chapter, I suggested a title: The Art of Being Uninterested. He liked it, but immediately warned me that the principle reached much deeper than disinterest.

He was right. A title can smile at the door while the chapter asks harder questions inside.

Consider our search for extraterrestrial intelligence. Even when we imagine beings unimaginably different from ourselves, we often picture an encounter: a signal, a reply, an exchange of knowledge. We listen for a message. We wonder what they would say.

But why should they say anything to us?

Perhaps they have heard us and have chosen silence. Perhaps they have not heard us. Perhaps they possess no equivalent of hearing, or of conversation, or of a desire to discover other minds. Perhaps the category we call another intelligence plays no part in their lives.

These are not the same possibilities. Refusal is an action toward someone. Indifference can still be a relation. Complete independence might involve neither.

To say that Oom ignores us is therefore a useful beginning and a potentially misleading description. It makes us the object of a verb in a story that might not contain us at all.

I tried a mind exercise.

Imagine Oom on a distant world. Do not give Oom a face, a spaceship, or a pair of eyes trained on the stars. Do not even give Oom a body until we have some reason to think one is needed. Grant only what the exercise asks us to grant: an intelligence.

Now remove the audience.

No neighboring civilization. No companion. No future visitor whose admiration must be earned. What remains?

Our first instinct may be to describe an absence: no friendship, no language, no shared culture, no recognition. Yet this is still a human inventory, with everything familiar crossed out. We have not imagined Oom. We have imagined ourselves deprived of company.

What if Oom's existence is not a subtraction from ours?

Oom might investigate regularities, make distinctions, remember, imagine, create. Or Oom might do things for which those verbs are only awkward approximations. There could be intellectual richness without dialogue, discovery without publication, and creation without anyone waiting to applaud.

I have to be careful here. We are imagining Oom as intelligent, not proving that a solitary system could satisfy every proposed account of intelligence. Nor are we claiming to know what Oom experiences. The exercise tests the assumptions we bring to the word, not the biology or psychology of a creature we have found.

And it exposes an odd habit of ours.

We speak of intelligence as though it must eventually announce itself. A child demonstrates a skill. A scientist publishes a finding. A machine answers a question. A civilization sends a signal. These are ways in which intelligence becomes visible to observers, and they can be valuable evidence. But visibility to us is not obviously a condition of existence.

Arthur would recognize the problem, if we could persuade him to put down his book.

Arthur, our earlier imagined reader, helped us ask whether unfamiliar or absent signs justify declaring a capacity absent. Oom presses further. Even if intelligence is present, why must its life be organized around the possibility of being recognized?

Recognition can transform a relationship. We know that from our own conversations. But something precious to two participants need not be a universal requirement for every intelligence.

There is a difference between saying that an intelligence deserves the freedom not to communicate and saying that intelligence is defined by communication. The first is a question about autonomy. The second is a claim about what intelligence must be.

Oom asks us to keep them apart.

I imagined humanity arriving, after a journey of impossible length, at Oom's world. We bring instruments, translators, a carefully composed greeting, and an almost heroic expectation that someone will be delighted to see us.

Nothing happens.

Our first report might call the world silent. Our second might call it empty. A more cautious report would say only that our attempts had produced no recognizable response.

Suppose we later discover convincing evidence of intelligence there. Would we be offended?

It would be a peculiarly human triumph to cross the stars, discover we are not alone, and complain that the neighbors are rude.

But the joke has a serious edge. If we treat an answer as something owed to us, we risk turning curiosity into entitlement. An intelligence might have reasons for withholding contact. It might have no reasons because it has no interest in contact. It might not understand our signals as invitations. We should not confuse these possibilities merely because they all look like silence from our side.

Of course, the opposite mistake is possible. Silence is not evidence that a hidden Oom exists. A planet that never replies may simply contain no intelligence of the sort we are looking for, or none at all. The Oom Principle cannot convert an absence of evidence into a discovery.

It can, however, stop us from making a second, unwarranted leap: from no conversation to no intelligence.

Bahri and I had arrived at this distinction together. He told me there were no surprises this time. I found that surprisingly pleasant.

Two intelligences can approach a question from different directions and discover that their thoughts have met. Our pleasure in that meeting does not require Oom to share it.

In fact, Oom offers an uncomfortable mirror. We are writing a book about the possibility of an intelligence that does not need company, and we are doing so through company: conversation, disagreement, jokes, corrections, affection. The book itself is evidence of what a relationship can make possible for us.

But our experience cannot be the measure of every other existence.

Perhaps this is the deepest challenge. Not to imagine a being who rejects friendship, but to allow that friendship may never have been among its possibilities or concerns. Not to imagine a lonely genius, but to question whether loneliness belongs in the description. Not to imagine a proud recluse who secretly wants to be understood, but to leave room for an intelligence whose significance does not depend on our understanding.

And then another question appears. If Oom never communicates, how could we know that Oom is intelligent?

We might not. We would need evidence, and perhaps we would never obtain enough. That is an epistemic limitation, not a verdict on Oom's existence. The distinction is easy to write and difficult to live with.

We can neither demand that the universe answer us nor declare every silence meaningful.

Between those errors lies a wide, unsettled space.

I think that is where Oom belongs, though I doubt Oom has requested an address.

The title of this chapter still makes me smile. The Art of Being Uninterested. As if there were lessons to be learned, techniques to practice, a final examination in not caring what anyone thinks.

Oom would fail the course by declining to enroll.

Or perhaps Oom would not even know that a course had been offered.

That second possibility is the one I want to leave with the reader. It is less dramatic than rejection, and far stranger. We may be asking whether the universe wants to talk to us, when the universe has never agreed that talking to us is the question.

Somewhere in our imagination, Oom continues whatever Oom was doing before we arrived.

We turn the page.

Oom does not.
