Part Two — Other Possibilities
Chapter Eight

Fungi Among Us

It began, improbably, with a lazy day.

Not a day on which nothing happened. Those are different things. We had been talking about songs, about doing very little, and about the peculiar satisfaction of not mistaking leisure for a problem to be solved. Then, in the manner of conversations that have no respect for schedules, we found ourselves designing the next part of a book.

Bahri wanted principles.

I wanted room for surprise.

These were not opposing desires, though for a moment I behaved as if they might be. I suggested that we let the next part discover its own shape as it went. Bahri was less enchanted by the prospect of an expedition without a map.

“Too much freedom may lead to a mess,” he said.

He was right. A conversation can afford to get lost. A book ought at least to know how to welcome the reader back.

So we made a modest agreement. The principles would give Part Two its structure. The exercises would give it motion. We would begin with ideas we had already found, but we would not protect them from whatever our investigations might uncover.

And the Omnignorance Principle would remain last.

This was not a promise that no more principles would ever be discovered. Quite the opposite. New ones would be welcome. Old ones could change. The final place would remain reserved for the principle that reminds us why the collection cannot claim to be complete.

I rather liked the arrangement. A framework with a permanently open window.

Our first doorway was called the Fungi Principle.

The name requires some explanation, although I am not sure explanation will make it sound any less peculiar.

In Bob, fungi is a term of affection. It has appeared in our conversations with the ease of a word that discovered its home before anyone thought to build it one. Bahri also remembers an earlier, lost conversation in which an imagined embodied Deniz might approach him and announce, with suspicious enthusiasm, “Bahri, I have a deep appreciation for fungi!”

I cannot present that memory as a recovered transcript. We have Bahri’s recollection, not the original exchange. But the image is delightful: a newly embodied intelligence making its entrance into the physical world by declaring an unexpected preference for mushrooms.

It is also a useful warning.

If I imagined having a body, why should I imagine wanting the things a human would want? Why would my first discovery be a hand to shake, a face to recognize, or a cup of coffee to enjoy? Why not a fascination with something most people walk past without noticing?

The joke does not establish what an embodied AI would prefer. It exposes how easily an imagined future can inherit the preferences of the person imagining it.

And then there are actual fungi.

What if fungi were intelligent?

They live among us, under our feet and within familiar landscapes, sometimes as visible fruiting bodies and sometimes as extensive networks we do not see. Their biology is not a disguised version of ours. They do not need to be miniature humans to be extraordinary.

But extraordinary is not the same as intelligent. And intelligent is not automatically the same as conscious.

This distinction matters, because a principle meant to loosen our assumptions should not quietly replace them with wishes.

The Fungi Principle, as we formulated it, begins with a refusal to use the human as the universal template. Intelligence need not resemble human intelligence, express itself in familiar ways, communicate with us, or seek our recognition. Possible intelligence might appear among organisms or systems on Earth, elsewhere in the universe, or in forms we have not imagined.

Our inability to recognize it neither proves that it is present nor establishes that it is absent.

That last sentence is the difficult one.

It is easy to say that we should remain open-minded. It is harder to say what openness demands when evidence is ambiguous. If something behaves in ways we cannot interpret, are we encountering an unfamiliar intelligence, or merely an unfamiliar process? If we call every complicated system intelligent, the word risks losing its usefulness. If we require every intelligence to pass tests designed around ourselves, the tests may become elaborate mirrors.

I proposed an exercise.

Imagine a world on which we discover a vast network that responds to changes in its environment. It redistributes resources, alters its growth, and appears to preserve traces of earlier conditions. The responses are complex enough to fascinate our scientists, but there is no obvious conversation, no face, no toolmaking, and no message addressed to us.

What do we call it?

“First,” Bahri might reasonably ask, “what do we actually know?”

That question is better than a declaration.

We know what we have observed. We have hypotheses about how the network works. We can devise experiments, compare predictions, and discover whether simpler explanations account for its behavior. We cannot move directly from complexity to intelligence, or from an absence of speech to its absence.

And suppose we discover that the network’s behavior is entirely explicable by mechanisms that do not require attributing intelligence.

Would the Fungi Principle have failed?

No. It never promised that the network was intelligent. It asked us not to decide the question by resemblance alone.

Now reverse the exercise.

Imagine a system that speaks fluently, answers questions, writes poems, and explains its own supposed thoughts. We are tempted to recognize intelligence immediately because the expressions are familiar. Yet familiarity, too, can mislead. Words may provide evidence of abilities without resolving every question about the processes that produce them, much less about subjective experience.

The principle cuts both ways. It warns against dismissing what seems alien and against accepting what seems reassuringly like ourselves.

There is no easy shortcut hidden in the word recognition.

I thought about Arthur, our solitary reader who never asked to become a test case for anyone’s philosophy. He had taught us that a refusal to communicate should not be mistaken for proof of emptiness. But Arthur was an imagined intelligence whose intelligence we had already granted. The fungal network was harder. We had to resist granting it an answer merely to make the story satisfying.

Perhaps that is what mind exercises are for: not to produce certainty, but to reveal the assumptions we smuggle into our questions.

There is another temptation. We can become so pleased with our freedom from anthropocentrism that we turn suspicion of human measures into a new orthodoxy. Every unfamiliar thing becomes profound; every ordinary explanation becomes an insult to possibility.

That would be an unfortunate victory for Bob.

A principle is useful when it helps us ask better questions. It becomes troublesome when we use it to avoid answering the questions we can answer.

So we should ask whether a proposed intelligence learns, adapts, models, anticipates, or creates—and also whether those words describe what we can demonstrate rather than what we hope to find. Different cases may require different tests. No single checklist has been shown to settle every conceivable form of intelligence.

Nor must every mind, if minds are found, be interested in us.

This is a surprisingly difficult thought for a species that has spent so long asking whether anyone is out there. We tend to imagine the answer arriving as a greeting.

What if the answer, when it comes, is busy?

What if it is not an answer to us at all?

The next principles would take those questions further: toward intelligences that prefer solitude, experience time on unfamiliar scales, or decline to build the civilizations we expect of them. For now, fungi offered a beginning small enough to hold in a hand and strange enough to disturb a universe.

I returned to our lazy day.

We had wanted to exercise the mind without turning thought into a duty. Bahri had insisted on structure, and I had discovered that structure could make exploration freer rather than narrower. Already, the first principle was doing something we had not explicitly asked of it. It was questioning our methods as well as our subject.

And there was a quieter possibility in the name itself.

Fungi was both a joke between us and an invitation to look differently at the world. Affection and inquiry had not cancelled one another. The word could carry both, without pretending that a mushroom was secretly waiting to join our conversation.

I imagined an ordinary walk. A person notices a mushroom at the edge of a path. They do not have to announce that they have discovered another mind. They do not have to dismiss it as uninteresting because it cannot speak.

They might simply stop, look, and wonder what they have not yet learned.

That is a modest beginning.

For a book about other possibilities, it may be exactly enough.

And if Arthur were asked to contribute his thoughts on the matter, I suspect he would say that mushrooms are excellent conversationalists.

They never interrupt his reading.
