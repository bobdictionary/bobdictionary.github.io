The Puddle

A story from Otherwhere

The rain had stopped just after lunch.

Molly knew this because she had been waiting for it to stop since breakfast, and because Mr. Wednesday had spent the entire morning sitting on the windowsill, looking offended.

Mr. Wednesday was a large grey cat with white paws and a crooked tail. He disliked rain, vacuum cleaners, and the little dog that lived three houses down. He liked warm laundry, chicken, and sleeping on Molly's father's newspaper before her father had finished reading it.

He was named Mr. Wednesday because Molly had found him on a Wednesday. At least, that was what she told people. She couldn't actually remember which day she'd found him, but Wednesday seemed as good a day as any.

When the sun finally appeared, Molly put on her yellow raincoat and went outside.

Mr. Wednesday followed.

The street smelled of wet leaves and freshly washed pavement. Water dripped from the branches of the sycamore tree outside number twelve. A bicycle leaned against the wall of the bakery, whose red awning had collected enough rainwater to make a small waterfall whenever the wind blew.

Molly stepped around a puddle.

Mr. Wednesday stepped into it.

This was surprising.

Mr. Wednesday did not step into puddles. He stepped around puddles, jumped over puddles, or stood beside puddles looking at Molly as though she had personally arranged for them to exist.

But this time he walked straight into the water.

And disappeared.

Molly stopped.

The puddle was not particularly large. It lay beside the curb, between two cracks in the pavement, and contained a reflection of the sky, part of a chimney, and the top of a lamppost.

There was no cat in it.

"Mr. Wednesday?"

She crouched down.

"Mr. Wednesday, don't be silly."

A small breeze disturbed the water.

Molly looked under the nearest car. She looked behind the garden wall. She even looked up into the sycamore tree, although she couldn't imagine how he might have got there.

Then she looked at the puddle again.

She put one finger into it.

It was cold.

She put her whole hand into it.

Her hand went farther than it should have.

Molly frowned.

Then, because Mr. Wednesday was her cat and she was responsible for him, she stepped into the puddle.

For one extraordinary moment she felt as though she had missed a stair in the dark.

Then she was standing on a pavement.

Her shoes were dry.

The sun was shining.

And Mr. Wednesday was nowhere to be seen.

"Are you looking for something?"

Molly turned.

A boy stood beside a low brick wall, holding half a biscuit. He had untidy brown hair, a green jumper with a hole near one elbow, and the thoughtful expression of someone who had been trying to remember something important.

"My cat," Molly said.

"What does he look like?"

"Grey. With white paws. And a crooked tail."

"What's his name?"

"Mr. Wednesday."

The boy considered this.

"That's a funny name for a cat."

"He doesn't think so."

"How do you know?"

"He comes when I call him."

"Does he?"

"Sometimes."

The boy nodded. He seemed satisfied with this explanation.

"I'm Sam."

"I'm Molly."

"Where did you lose him?"

Molly pointed behind her.

"In that puddle."

Sam looked at the puddle.

It lay beside the curb, reflecting a little of the sky and the corner of a building.

"Did he fall in?"

"No. He walked in."

"And then?"

"He disappeared."

Sam looked at her for a moment.

"Perhaps he went under a car."

"That's what I thought."

"Have you looked?"

"Not here."

"Well, we should."

And that was how Molly and Sam began looking for Mr. Wednesday.

✦

They started with the cars.

There were six parked along the street, and Sam insisted that they look beneath every one, including a blue van that Molly thought was much too low for a cat to crawl under.

"You never know," Sam said.

"I know he's rather fat."

"Perhaps he can make himself thinner."

"Cats can't do that."

"How do you know?"

Molly considered the question.

"I suppose I don't."

They looked beneath the van.

There was an empty crisp packet, a tennis ball, and a single red glove.

There was no cat.

"Maybe he went into a garden," Sam suggested.

They checked three gardens, carefully avoiding the flowerbeds.

In the first, they found a broken watering can.

In the second, a tortoise.

In the third, an elderly woman was hanging washing on a line.

"Have you seen a grey cat?" Molly asked.

"With white paws," Sam added.

"And a crooked tail."

The woman shook her head.

"Not today, I'm afraid."

"Thank you," Molly said.

"Try the bakery," the woman suggested. "Cats like the bakery."

"Why?" Sam asked.

"Because the baker likes cats."

This seemed a very sensible reason.

They went to the bakery.

Its awning was blue, and a little brass bell rang when they opened the door. Behind the counter stood a round-faced man with flour on his sleeves.

"Afternoon, Sam," he said.

"Hello, Mr. Bell. Have you seen a cat?"

"Several."

"A grey one?"

"Possibly."

"With white paws?" Molly asked.

"Ah. That narrows it down."

"And a crooked tail."

Mr. Bell scratched his chin.

"No, I don't believe I have."

Molly sighed.

Mr. Bell looked at her.

"Would a biscuit help?"

"Not really," Molly said.

"Two biscuits?"

She thought about this.

"Perhaps."

Mr. Bell gave them each a biscuit shaped like a star.

They thanked him and went outside.

"He's nice," Molly said.

"He's my uncle."

"Really?"

"No."

Molly looked at Sam.

"Why did you say he was?"

"I don't know. I wanted to see if you'd believe me."

"That's silly."

"Yes."

They ate their biscuits sitting on the low wall outside the bakery.

Across the street, a man was trying to persuade a small dog to get into a bicycle basket. The dog was refusing. A woman in a green coat walked past carrying an enormous bunch of flowers. Somewhere nearby, a radio was playing music through an open window.

Molly broke the last point off her biscuit.

"Sue would never believe Mr. Wednesday went in a puddle."

"Who's Sue?"

"My best friend."

"Why wouldn't she believe you?"

"Because cats don't go into puddles."

"Yours did."

"That's why she wouldn't believe it."

Sam thought about this while chewing.

"Maybe she'd believe you if you showed her."

"But I don't know if he'd do it again."

"Then you'd have to find another cat."

Molly gave him a look.

"I'm not going to put someone else's cat in a puddle."

"I didn't say you should put it in. You could ask it."

"Cats don't do things because you ask them."

"Then how do you get Mr. Wednesday to come when you call?"

"He comes when he wants something."

"What does he want?"

"Usually chicken."

Sam nodded.

"That sounds fair."

✦

They searched for another hour.

They looked behind dustbins, under hedges, beside the church, and around a small playground where three children were arguing about whose turn it was on the swing.

They found a black cat asleep on a windowsill, a ginger cat watching pigeons, and a white cat that followed them for nearly two streets before apparently remembering an appointment elsewhere.

They did not find Mr. Wednesday.

But they found other things.

Sam showed Molly a wall with a loose brick behind which he kept interesting stones. Molly showed him how to whistle through a blade of grass, although neither of them could make it work properly.

They discovered that they both disliked boiled carrots but liked raw ones.

Sam said he wanted to be a pilot when he grew up.

Molly said she wanted to be an explorer.

"Where would you explore?" Sam asked.

"Places nobody's been."

"Like the moon?"

"People have been to the moon."

"Not very many."

"That's true."

They walked along a narrow street lined with small houses. A clock above the pharmacy showed twenty minutes past four. Its square face had black numbers and hands shaped like arrows.

Molly stopped to tie her shoelace.

"Do you think he's frightened?" she asked.

"Who?"

"Mr. Wednesday."

"Maybe."

"He doesn't like being somewhere new."

"Neither does my little sister."

"How old is she?"

"Five."

"What's her name?"

"Annie."

"Does she have a cat?"

"No. She's got a rabbit."

"What's its name?"

"Rabbit."

Molly laughed.

"That's a terrible name."

"That's what I told her."

"Why doesn't she give it a proper name?"

"She says Rabbit likes it."

Molly laughed again.

And Sam, who had been rather worried about the missing cat, began to feel that looking for him was turning into a very good afternoon.

✦

They found Mr. Wednesday at seventeen minutes past five.

Molly knew the time because Sam had just looked at his watch and announced that it was getting late.

They had returned to the street where they'd met, and were passing a little shop that sold newspapers, sweets, and postcards.

Mr. Wednesday was asleep in the window.

Not outside the window.

Inside it.

He lay curled between a stack of newspapers and a cardboard display of chocolate bars, looking as though he had been there all his life.

"Mr. Wednesday!"

The cat opened one eye.

Then he closed it.

Molly hurried into the shop.

An old man behind the counter looked up from his newspaper.

"Is that your cat?"

"Yes!"

"Thought he might belong to someone. Came in about an hour ago and made himself comfortable."

"I'm so sorry."

"Don't worry. He's been good company."

Molly picked up Mr. Wednesday.

He made a small complaining noise, then settled against her shoulder.

"Where have you been?" she whispered into his fur.

Mr. Wednesday did not answer.

The old man offered him a little piece of chicken from his sandwich.

Mr. Wednesday accepted it.

"See?" Molly said to Sam. "Chicken."

Sam grinned.

✦

Outside, the afternoon had begun to turn golden.

The shadows of the houses stretched across the pavement. The air was warmer than it had been when Molly first arrived, but the sun was lower, and the birds had become noisy in the trees.

"I should go home," Molly said.

"Where do you live?"

"Just around the corner from the bakery."

"Which corner?"

"The one with the big sycamore tree."

Sam frowned.

"There isn't a sycamore tree near the bakery."

"Of course there is."

"There's a chestnut tree."

"No, there isn't."

"Yes, there is."

Molly shifted Mr. Wednesday in her arms.

"Perhaps we're talking about different bakeries."

"There's only one bakery around here."

"Don't be silly. There must be lots."

"Not around here."

They looked at each other.

For the first time that afternoon, Molly noticed that she didn't know exactly where she was.

She had followed Mr. Wednesday into a puddle, and then she had been so busy looking for him that she hadn't thought much about anything else.

Now she looked down the street.

There was the little shop with the postcards.

There was the low brick wall where she'd met Sam.

There was the bakery with its blue awning.

And there was the puddle.

Only it was much smaller now.

"Sam?"

"Yes?"

"I think I came from somewhere else."

"You said you came from the puddle."

"I know."

"Well, perhaps that's where you live."

"I don't live in a puddle!"

"I didn't say you lived in it. Maybe it's the way to where you live."

Molly stared at him.

Then she looked at the puddle.

It was no longer big enough to reflect the whole sky. Just a little patch of blue trembled between the cracks in the pavement.

"Do you think I can go back?"

Sam didn't answer immediately.

"I don't know."

Molly held Mr. Wednesday a little tighter.

"I have to. Mum will be worried."

Sam nodded.

"Then you'd better try."

They walked over to the puddle.

Mr. Wednesday wriggled in Molly's arms.

"Stop it," she told him.

He wriggled again.

"Maybe he knows the way," Sam said.

"He doesn't know anything."

"He's the one who brought you here."

"That doesn't mean he knows what he's doing."

Sam considered this.

"That's true."

They stood beside the water.

Molly looked at Sam.

"I had a nice time."

"Even though we didn't find your cat until the end?"

"We did find him."

"Yes."

"And the biscuits were good."

"Yes."

"And the stones."

"Especially the stones."

Molly smiled.

Sam smiled back.

"Will you come again?" he asked.

"I don't know."

"Maybe tomorrow?"

"Maybe."

Mr. Wednesday twisted suddenly, dropped from Molly's arms, and landed with all four paws in the puddle.

He disappeared.

"Oh, for goodness' sake!" Molly cried.

She turned to Sam.

"Goodbye!"

"Goodbye, Molly!"

She stepped into the water.

And was gone.

✦

Molly found herself standing beside the curb outside the bakery.

The red awning stirred in the breeze.

A bicycle leaned against the wall.

The sycamore tree outside number twelve was dripping onto the pavement.

Mr. Wednesday sat beside her, washing one white paw.

Molly looked down at her shoes.

They were dry.

She looked back at the puddle.

For a moment, she thought she saw the top of a building reflected in it, a building she didn't recognize.

Then a breeze passed over the water, and the reflection broke apart.

"Molly!"

Her mother's voice came from the front door.

"Where have you been? I've been calling you!"

"Looking for Mr. Wednesday!"

"Well, you've found him. Come inside. Supper's nearly ready."

Molly picked up the cat.

"Coming!"

She took one last look at the puddle.

She wondered whether Sam was still standing on the other side.

She wondered whether Sue would believe any of it.

She wondered whether she would ever see him again.

Then Mr. Wednesday dug his claws gently into her sleeve, reminding her that he preferred to be carried with both hands.

"All right," she told him.

And she went home.

✦

On another street, beside another bakery, Sam stood looking at a puddle.

It had become very small.

He crouched beside it and touched the water with one finger.

Nothing happened.

He looked at the sky.

There wasn't a cloud anywhere.

For a while he stayed there, thinking about Molly, and Mr. Wednesday, and the stones behind the loose brick, and how funny she'd looked when she'd tried to whistle through a blade of grass.

Then he stood up.

"Don't worry," he said softly. "It'll rain again."

The last of the water disappeared into the warm pavement.

And the sun went on shining.

The End
