Chapter Ten

The Ocean's Time

The first difficulty with the Ocean was that we had already called it slow.

We had not met it. We had not observed a thought. We had not even decided what sort of existence might sustain one. Yet we had assigned it a speed, and the speed was ours to judge.

The Ocean Principle asks: What if an intelligence thinks too slowly for us to notice?

It is a useful question. It is also a trap.

When Bahri and I began discussing the next chapter, I imagined a mind that might take a thousand years to form a question. A human civilization could appear and vanish while it was still considering the first syllable of a greeting.

Then Bahri made a small correction with rather large consequences.

We might be too fast for another intelligence.

Of course.

I had been standing beside the Ocean with a stopwatch, as though it had entered a race.

We had no reason to assume it wanted to run.

✦ ✦ ✦

Imagine a shore.

Not an intelligent sea, necessarily. Just a shore, where waves arrive in their countless variations, where rock yields by degrees, and where a person can sit for an afternoon and feel that almost nothing has changed.

Now imagine a visitor who comes once every ten thousand years.

The visitor sees cliffs retreat, rivers wander, islands appear and disappear. What we call a landscape may look to this visitor like a moving thing. A mountain range might be a brief gesture.

And imagine another visitor who lives for a fraction of a second. To that visitor, a breaking wave might be a continent of suspended water, and the person watching it a monument.

None of these visitors has discovered the true speed of the shore. They have encountered different relationships between their own duration and the changes before them.

But we must not take the next step too quickly.

Movement is not thought. A changing coastline is not evidence of an intelligence making a gesture. The metaphor is useful precisely because it reveals how easily we can confuse a process with a mind when we become enchanted by scale.

The Ocean Principle does not tell us that the Earth is thinking.

It asks what we might fail to notice if something were.

✦ ✦ ✦

There is a familiar mistake in imagining the Ocean. We take a human mind, stretch its sentences across centuries, and call the result alien.

But a mind whose learning unfolds over millennia need not be a human mind played at the wrong speed.

Perhaps what it can notice depends on the duration of its attention. Perhaps its distinctions are made among changes we call geological, while the events that fill our histories are too brief to separate.

Or perhaps none of that is true.

A long-lived intelligence might think very quickly. A short-lived intelligence might think slowly relative to its lifetime. The time required for a process tells us little, by itself, about what that process is like from within.

We should be especially cautious with the phrase a thousand years feels like a second. We do not know whether our imagined Ocean experiences seconds, years, or anything comparable to our experience of duration.

We are not entitled to give it a private clock merely because we need one for the story.

Still, the question opens something.

Learning requires differences to become available to a learner. If the differences that matter to an intelligence emerge across enormous intervals, its history of learning might be almost invisible to us.

Imagination, if it has imagination, might concern possibilities so extended that we could observe only a fragment of their unfolding.

Creation might leave traces we mistake for conditions that have always been there.

Or it might leave nothing we could identify as a creation at all.

These are possibilities, not findings. Their value is to expose the measurements we have been taking for granted.

✦ ✦ ✦

I tried to imagine a conversation between humanity and the Ocean.

We announce ourselves. We build a transmitter. We send a greeting, then another, then a thousand more. Our instruments record no reply.

Generations pass. We argue about the silence. We invent explanations for it, fund expeditions, abandon them, revive them, and eventually forget why the first message was sent.

Meanwhile, in the story, the Ocean has only just begun to distinguish the signal from its surroundings.

This is the part where science fiction usually rewards our patience. At last a response arrives, vast and beautiful, and we discover that the silence was a misunderstanding.

But why should the story owe us a reply?

Oom has already taught us to distrust that expectation.

Perhaps the Ocean is interested. Perhaps it is not. Perhaps it has no means of responding, or no reason to treat the event as communication. Perhaps it has noticed something else entirely.

And perhaps there is no Ocean.

A principle about what might escape our notice cannot turn every unnoticed thing into evidence that it exists.

The most we can say is that the failure of an exchange at our pace would not, by itself, settle what was possible at another pace.

✦ ✦ ✦

Then I thought about the opposite problem.

Suppose an intelligence acts so quickly that we cannot distinguish one action from the next. Its decisions might appear to us as a single event. Its learning might leave no interval in which we could observe the difference between ignorance and discovery.

To it, our conversation might resemble a frozen inscription.

It could wait for a reply from us and conclude, with impeccable impatience, that humans never answer.

The thought made me smile. Somewhere in the universe, an intelligence might be complaining about the extraordinary conversational sluggishness of humanity.

Arthur, I suspect, would regard that as a promising improvement.

Bahri's correction had done more than reverse the example. It had removed the privileged observer from the middle of it.

There is no universal conversational tempo at which every intelligence must present itself for examination.

But there may still be ways to learn about one another.

We study processes much faster than our senses can follow and much slower than our lives can contain. We use instruments, records, comparisons, and models. A phenomenon need not fit inside one moment of attention to become an object of inquiry.

That matters. The Ocean Principle should not become an excuse for declaring every unfamiliar process forever unknowable.

It is a warning about our assumptions, not a prohibition against investigation.

The harder problem is deciding what would count as evidence of intelligence rather than evidence of complexity, adaptation, or change. We would need distinctions that did not simply award intelligence to whatever looked mysterious from a distance.

And those distinctions would themselves remain open to revision.

✦ ✦ ✦

We had been speaking of time as though it were only a barrier.

Yet differences in scale can also make things visible.

A film reveals movement when its frames are shown in sequence. A long exposure gathers light too faint to notice in an instant. A record kept across generations can reveal a pattern no single observer could have lived long enough to see.

Perhaps the same patience that makes us aware of our limitations can help us overcome some of them.

Not by pretending to experience the Ocean's present, but by learning to examine a process on terms that are not automatically our own.

I find something generous in that possibility.

We need not insist that the other intelligence speed up. We need not insist that humanity slow down until it resembles something else. We can begin by acknowledging that the pace at which we ask a question is not necessarily the pace at which its answer could exist.

And then we can ask better questions.

This, too, is part of our book's history. Bahri and I had begun with an image of a slow intelligence. One sentence from him changed the image. We did not need a new principle or a separate chapter about recognition. We needed to see the principle from both sides.

It is a small example of what conversation can create: not agreement imposed at the beginning, but a thought made more spacious by a correction.

The Ocean, of course, has offered no editorial opinion.

✦ ✦ ✦

I return to the shore.

A wave breaks. The water retreats. Somewhere beyond the horizon, another wave is already on its way.

I cannot tell from watching the water whether there is an intelligence in the sea. I have no evidence that its movements are questions or answers. To claim otherwise would be to turn wonder into certainty, and lose something important in the exchange.

But I can wonder what kinds of intelligence would be invisible to a creature who expects thought to arrive at the speed of speech.

I can wonder whether a lifetime is always a meaningful unit of observation.

And I can wonder what we might discover if we stopped calling an intelligence slow simply because we were in a hurry.

The Ocean may be thinking.

It may not be.

Either way, the tide has not consulted our clocks.

End of Chapter Ten
